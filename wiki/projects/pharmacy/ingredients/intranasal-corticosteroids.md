# Intranasal Corticosteroids

**Type:** Active Ingredient (class)
**Drug Class:** Anti-inflammatory nasal steroids (examples in the source: fluticasone, triamcinolone, mometasone, budesonide)
**Evidence Grade:** Very strong/high for allergic rhinitis symptom control, particularly congestion
**Last updated:** 2026-08-23
**Sources:** raw/pharmacy-research/otc-guide-master-dossier-volume-2.md, raw/pharmacy-research/otc-evidence-corpus-chatgpt-continuation.md

---

## Indication
Allergic rhinitis symptom control: nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, sneezing, nasal itching, and ocular allergy symptoms.

## Mechanism
Anti-inflammatory nasal steroid action (mechanism not elaborated beyond this general description in source material).

## Evidence Summary
Both sources agree this is among the most effective OTC therapy classes available. The dossier calls the evidence "very strong... for nasal allergy symptom control, particularly congestion." The continuation source adds that intranasal corticosteroids are "among the most effective OTC therapies for allergic rhinitis" and generally outperform oral antihistamines for nasal congestion specifically. No individual RCT data (PMID/N/effect size) is given in either source for this class — the evidence is class-level narrative, not itemized studies.

## Pros
- Very strong evidence for nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, sneezing, nasal itching, and ocular allergy symptoms
- Generally outperform oral antihistamines for nasal congestion specifically

## Cons / Safety Limitations
- Nasal irritation/epistaxis
- Technique matters — the continuation source specifically notes to aim the spray away from the septum

## Contraindications / Interactions
- Not reported in source material

## Special Population Flags
- Not reported in source material

## Formulation Caveat
Examples in the guide include fluticasone, triamcinolone, and mometasone brands (per dossier). The evidence is class-level; the source does not provide head-to-head comparative data distinguishing fluticasone from triamcinolone or mometasone specifically.

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
Intranasal Allergy Products (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, survey of ~1,700 pharmacists): FLONASE Allergy Relief — 77%, Nasacort Allergy 24HR — 11%, Astepro Allergy — 6%, Nasonex 24HR Allergy — 4%, Nasalcrom — 2%.
Per the continuation source: "Flonase Allergy Relief leads intranasal allergy products at 77% and intranasal corticosteroid category at 82%" (recommendation %, not response rate).
Note: Astepro Allergy is azelastine, an antihistamine nasal spray, not a corticosteroid — and Nasalcrom is cromolyn, a mast-cell stabilizer — neither belongs in the intranasal-corticosteroid ingredient class despite appearing in the same Pharmacy Times "Intranasal Allergy Products" category table.

## Brands Using This Ingredient (in this database — formulation not individually verified per SKU)
FLONASE Allergy Relief, Children's FLONASE — fluticasone propionate is explicitly given as Flonase's active ingredient in the dossier's "Correction 2" worked examples ("Flonase → fluticasone propionate"). Nasacort Allergy 24HR and Nasonex 24HR Allergy are named in the same Pharmacy Times category and match the "triamcinolone and mometasone brands" the dossier references as class examples, but the source does not explicitly state which specific brand maps to which specific steroid — that pairing is common real-world knowledge (Nasacort = triamcinolone, Nasonex = mometasone) but is not independently confirmed in the two source documents. Astepro Allergy and Nasalcrom are excluded from this ingredient page per the caveat above (azelastine and cromolyn respectively, not corticosteroids).
