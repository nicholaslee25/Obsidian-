# Oxymetazoline

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Topical alpha-adrenergic decongestant (nasal spray)
**Evidence Grade:** Moderate-to-high for short-term relief; the major limitation is rebound congestion with prolonged use, not lack of initial efficacy
**Last updated:** 2026-08-23
**Sources:** raw/pharmacy-research/otc-guide-master-dossier-volume-2.md, raw/pharmacy-research/otc-evidence-corpus-chatgpt-continuation.md

---

## Indication
Short-term (per source guidance, traditionally limited to ~3 days) relief of nasal congestion.

## Mechanism
Topical alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction of nasal blood vessels.

## Evidence Summary
Randomized trials support rapid, temporary congestion relief — per the continuation source, oxymetazoline is "very effective for short-term congestion." The major problem is rhinitis medicamentosa (rebound congestion) with prolonged use; traditional guidance limits use to approximately 3 days. This is described as "one of the most important nasal-spray counseling points" in the source. No individual RCT data (PMID/N/effect size) is given in either source for oxymetazoline — this is class-level narrative evidence.

## Pros
- Very effective for short-term nasal congestion relief per both sources
- Rapid onset

## Cons / Safety Limitations
- Rebound congestion/rhinitis medicamentosa with prolonged use — the central safety issue for this ingredient
- Traditional guidance limits use to ~3 days

## Contraindications / Interactions
- Not reported in source material

## Special Population Flags
- Not reported in source material

## Formulation Caveat
Separate nasal-spray efficacy from oral decongestant evidence (pseudoephedrine, oral phenylephrine) — these are pharmacologically related but evaluated as distinct products with distinct evidence bases and distinct safety profiles (rebound congestion is specific to topical/nasal use, not oral decongestants).

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
Decongestants, Nasal Spray (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, survey of ~1,700 pharmacists): Afrin — 62%, NasalCrom — 10%, Dristan Nasal Spray — 6%, Neo-Synephrine — 5%, 4-Way Nasal Spray — 4%, Mucinex Nasal Spray — 4%, Vicks Sinex Ultra Fine Mist — 3%, Zicam — 3%, Xlear — 3%.
Note: the source does not specify which of these brands are oxymetazoline-based specifically versus other decongestant actives (e.g., NasalCrom is cromolyn, a mast-cell stabilizer, not a decongestant vasoconstrictor) — see brand caveat below.

## Brands Using This Ingredient (in this database — formulation not individually verified per SKU)
Afrin is the best-known oxymetazoline nasal spray brand in the real-world market and is the dominant product (62%) in the source's Decongestants, Nasal Spray category, but the source itself does not explicitly state Afrin's active ingredient. Per the database rule against inferring ingredient content from name/category alone, this link is noted with that caveat rather than asserted as fully confirmed. NasalCrom is very likely cromolyn sodium (a different mechanism entirely — a mast-cell stabilizer, not a decongestant) based on its real-world identity and its explicit mention as a separate agent in the source's "Priority ingredients" list — do not assume it is oxymetazoline. Vicks Sinex Ultra Fine Mist, Dristan Nasal Spray, Neo-Synephrine, and 4-Way Nasal Spray are not individually ingredient-confirmed in the source. Mucinex Nasal Spray is explicitly called out on the guaifenesin.md page as "a separate formulation (nasal decongestant)... not covered by [the] guaifenesin evidence" — consistent with this page's decongestant framing, though its exact active ingredient (oxymetazoline vs. another decongestant) is not confirmed in source.
