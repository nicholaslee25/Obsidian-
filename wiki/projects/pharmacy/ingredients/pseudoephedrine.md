# Pseudoephedrine

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Sympathomimetic (oral) decongestant
**Evidence Grade:** Moderate-to-high for short-term nasal decongestion, depending on population and endpoint
**Last updated:** 2026-08-23
**Sources:** raw/pharmacy-research/otc-guide-master-dossier-volume-2.md

---

## Indication
Short-term relief of nasal congestion associated with the common cold.

## Mechanism
Alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction of nasal blood vessels, reducing nasal mucosal swelling. Substantially stronger evidence base than oral phenylephrine (see that ingredient's page).

## Evidence Summary
Multiple double-blind, placebo-controlled trials support a real short-term decongestant effect, measured both objectively (nasal-airway resistance, acoustic rhinometry, nasal volume) and subjectively (symptom scores). The effect is consistent enough across studies to be considered the best-supported oral decongestant, but it is not uniformly positive — several trials found objective improvement without matching subjective symptom improvement, or vice versa, and one 3-day trial found the prespecified primary endpoint was not significantly different from placebo. Not established: meaningful shortening of the underlying viral illness, or prevention of colds.

### Studies

**Taverner, Danz & Economos, 1999**
- PMID: 10196648, DOI: 10.1046/j.1365-2273.1999.00208.x
- Design: Double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, single-dose
- Population: 54 previously healthy people with common cold, symptoms ≤5 days, moderate-to-severe nasal congestion (52 completed)
- Comparator/Dose: Pseudoephedrine 60 mg single oral dose vs. placebo
- Measurements: Subjective congestion, acoustic rhinometry, nasal minimum cross-sectional area, nasal volume, active posterior rhinomanometry
- Result: Subjective congestion improved significantly at 60/90/120/150 min post-dose. Total nasal minimum cross-sectional area increased vs. placebo (P=0.018). Nasal volume increased vs. placebo (P=0.003). Active posterior rhinomanometry showed no significant difference.
- Interpretation: Direct evidence a 60 mg oral dose can temporarily improve congestion in adults with the common cold, though objective and subjective measures were not uniformly positive.

**Jawad & Eccles, 1998**
- PMID: 9695162
- Design: Randomized controlled trial, N=40 (pseudoephedrine n=20, placebo n=20), two doses separated by 4 hours
- Comparator/Dose: Pseudoephedrine 60 mg
- Result: Objective nasal-airflow benefit over 7 hours with pseudoephedrine.
- Limitations: Small sample; objective airflow endpoint does not automatically equal meaningful improvement in all cold symptoms.

**Latte et al., 2004**
- PMID: 15236629, DOI: 10.1111/j.1440-1681.2004.04013.x
- Design: Double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, N=48 adults with acute coryza, single 60 mg dose
- Result: Nasal airway resistance decreased significantly (P=0.005, 95% CI 0.073–0.383 for the reported treatment effect). Nasal volume increased but did not reach significance (P=0.07). No significant change in minimum cross-sectional area or subjective nasal-congestion symptom score.
- Interpretation: Moderate objective decongestant effect; an important counterexample to overclaiming subjective benefit from every pseudoephedrine trial.

**Eccles et al., 2005**
- PMID: 15794071
- Design: Double-blind, randomized, parallel-group, placebo-controlled; N=238 patients with acute URI, mean age ~20 years; single dose day 1, repeated dosing through day 3
- Dose: Pseudoephedrine 60 mg
- Primary endpoint: Area under the nasal-airway-resistance (NAR) curve
- Result: Day 1 NAR AUC 0–3h significantly lower with pseudoephedrine (P=0.006); 0–4h (P=0.001). Day 3 NAR AUC 0–3h and 0–4h both P<0.001. Day 1 subjective congestion VAS AUC 0–3h (P=0.029), 0–4h (P=0.021). Day 3 subjective differences not significant. Overall congestion/stuffiness summary score better with pseudoephedrine (P=0.016).
- Interpretation: Stronger support for short-term decongestion than many other oral decongestant studies; repeated dosing may maintain objective benefit while subjective differences can diminish.

**Gelotte et al., 2019 (children)**
- PMID: 31274197, DOI: 10.1002/jcph.1472
- Design: Multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized
- Population: Children 6–11 years
- Dose: Pseudoephedrine HCl 30 mg tablets (current OTC monograph dose), up to 7 days as needed
- Primary endpoint: Weighted change from baseline in instantaneous nasal-congestion severity, 1–8 hours after first dose
- Result: Pseudoephedrine superior to placebo; least-squares mean treatment difference 1.2 (P=0.029)
- Adverse events: Somnolence — pseudoephedrine 71.9% vs. placebo 63.9%; insomnia — 34.4% vs. 38.9%; nervousness — 20.0% vs. 23.6%
- Interpretation: Demonstrates short-term pediatric decongestant efficacy at the studied age/dose; the high reported adverse-event percentages in both arms illustrate why AE definitions and baseline symptoms matter.

**Latte & Taverner, 2007**
- PMID: 17882915, DOI: 10.2500/ajr.2007.21.3041
- Design: Double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, N=216, three days
- Dose: Pseudoephedrine 60 mg four times daily
- Result: After first dose, small improvements across objective congestion measures. Prespecified primary endpoint (day-1 NAR AUC) was not significantly different. Total nasal volume increased 5.5% (P=0.031) during 0.5–3h. Pooled day 1/day 3 VAS decrease was 7.0% (P=0.072).
- Interpretation: Important negative/weak result — shows pseudoephedrine's objective vasoconstrictor effect does not always translate into a statistically significant symptom benefit.

**Pseudoephedrine + acetaminophen (combination trial)**
- PMID: 11115196
- Design: Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; N=430 (active n=216, placebo n=214)
- Population: Cold symptoms ≤48 hours, at least moderate overall "sinus" symptoms
- Result: Two hours after second dose, overall sinus symptom score reduction was greater with active (−1.30 ± 0.06) vs. placebo (−0.93 ± 0.06), P≤0.029; weighted sinus pressure/pain/congestion also greater with active (−1.14 ± 0.06 vs. −0.84 ± 0.06), P≤0.029.
- Interpretation: This is a combination-product trial, not pure pseudoephedrine evidence — supports short-term symptom relief of congestion/pressure/pain, but does not prove antiviral activity, shortened illness, prevention, or superiority over either component alone for every symptom.

**Aspirin + pseudoephedrine (combination trial)**
- PMID: 15500197
- Design: Multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; N=643 patients with acute URTI
- Interventions: ASA 1000 mg + pseudoephedrine 60 mg; ASA 500 mg + pseudoephedrine 30 mg; paracetamol 1000 mg + pseudoephedrine 60 mg; placebo
- Primary endpoint: AUC of nasal-congestion score during first 2 hours
- Result: All active treatments statistically superior to placebo on the primary endpoint; benefit remained significant up to 6 hours. Lower-dose ASA/pseudoephedrine did not show significant benefit in some subgroup analyses, particularly with severe or moderate baseline congestion depending on the comparison.
- Interpretation: Another combination-product trial — strong for a defined symptom endpoint while not proving the whole multi-symptom product shortens a viral illness.

**Ibuprofen + pseudoephedrine — pharmacy customer survey (real-world, not RCT)**
- Citation: Klimek L, et al., PMID 27925348, DOI 10.1111/ijcp.12907
- Design: Post-hoc analysis of an anonymous survey, N=1,770 pharmacy customers. This is NOT a randomized controlled trial — it is real-world observational evidence.
- Use: Useful for patient-reported outcomes and real-world patterns, but a lower evidence grade than RCTs; industry-affiliated authors were involved.

### Pseudoephedrine evidence synthesis (per source)
- Best-supported benefit: short-term nasal congestion relief; strongest evidence is for objective nasal patency and short-term symptom improvement
- Not established: meaningful shortening of the underlying viral illness; prevention of colds
- Important adverse effects: insomnia, nervousness, somnolence in some pediatric studies, clinically relevant cardiovascular stimulation

## Pros
- Best-supported oral decongestant in the source material — consistent objective and (usually) subjective improvement in nasal congestion across multiple RCTs, including a pediatric trial
- Substantially stronger evidence than oral phenylephrine

## Cons / Safety Limitations
- Can increase blood pressure/heart rate, cause insomnia and anxiety
- Effect on subjective symptom scores is not always statistically significant even when objective nasal measures improve (see Latte & Taverner 2007)
- Systematic review of OTC misuse (PMID 34025478; 92 articles, focused on antihistamines, dextromethorphan/codeine-containing cough medicines, and pseudoephedrine) found this evidence base important because OTC availability does not eliminate toxicity or misuse risk

## Contraindications / Interactions
- Not reported in source material beyond the cardiovascular/CNS stimulant effects noted above

## Special Population Flags
- Pediatric (6–11 years): Gelotte 2019 shows efficacy but also substantial somnolence/insomnia/nervousness rates in both active and placebo arms
- Regulated behind-the-counter in the US

## Formulation Caveat
Evidence here applies to oral pseudoephedrine itself. The combination-product trials (with acetaminophen, aspirin, or ibuprofen) are separate evidence and should not be read as proving efficacy of every multi-symptom product containing pseudoephedrine.

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
Decongestants, Oral (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, survey of ~1,700 pharmacists): Sudafed (pseudoephedrine) — 45%; Sudafed PE (phenylephrine) — 4% (listed separately in the source, confirming the two are tracked as distinct ingredients). This is a recommendation share, not a response rate.
