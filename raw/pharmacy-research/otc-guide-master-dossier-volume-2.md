# Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide --- Expanded Evidence Dossier

**Scope:** 2026 OTC Guide categories and pharmacist-recommended
products, expanded beyond the four homeopathic flu products.

**Important:** Pharmacy Times says the 2026 survey covered **145 product
categories** and was based on a national survey of 1,700 pharmacists.
The public OTC Guide taxonomy pages I could retrieve expose **142 named
categories** across the 14 visible sections; I am not inventing the
remaining three categories. The site describes the survey as pharmacist
recommendations, not efficacy rankings. citeturn3search1turn3search2

## How this dossier is being built

For each product, the eventual evidence record should distinguish:

1.  Exact commercial product/formulation.
2.  Active ingredient(s).
3.  Clinical indication.
4.  Best randomized trials.
5.  Systematic reviews/meta-analyses.
6.  Absolute response/success rates when available.
7.  Relative effects and confidence intervals.
8.  Duration of benefit.
9.  Adverse events.
10. Contraindications/interactions.
11. Cost if a study actually reports it.
12. Evidence quality.
13. Whether the evidence applies to the exact commercial product.

**Critical rule:** a pharmacist recommendation percentage is **not** a
clinical success rate.

# Part III --- Evidence by active ingredient / treatment class

This section is intentionally organized by active ingredient rather than
brand. A brand can have several formulations, and a study of one
formulation does not automatically validate every formulation sold under
the same brand.

## Acetaminophen

**Class:** Analgesic/antipyretic\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Pain and fever

### Evidence summary

Strong evidence for short-term analgesia and fever reduction; efficacy
is symptom-specific rather than antiviral.

### Cons / safety limitations

Important dose-dependent hepatotoxicity risk, especially with overdose
or duplicate acetaminophen-containing combination products.

### Product/formulation caveat

Use research on acetaminophen for the symptom being treated; do not
infer efficacy of a branded combination as a whole.

## Ibuprofen

**Class:** NSAID\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Pain, fever, inflammation

### Evidence summary

Strong randomized evidence for acute pain and fever; generally effective
at OTC doses.

### Cons / safety limitations

GI irritation/bleeding, renal risk, fluid retention and cardiovascular
considerations; NSAID contraindications matter.

### Product/formulation caveat

Combination cold products add decongestant risks and duplication issues.

## Naproxen

**Class:** NSAID\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Pain, inflammation

### Evidence summary

Strong evidence for acute musculoskeletal pain and dysmenorrhea; longer
duration than ibuprofen.

### Cons / safety limitations

GI bleeding, renal and cardiovascular risks; avoid duplicate NSAIDs.

### Product/formulation caveat

Research on naproxen does not establish benefit for the other
ingredients in a combination product.

## Pseudoephedrine

**Class:** Sympathomimetic decongestant\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Nasal congestion

### Evidence summary

Controlled trials support short-term nasal-decongestant effects.

### Cons / safety limitations

Can increase blood pressure/heart rate, cause insomnia and anxiety;
regulated behind-the-counter in the US.

### Product/formulation caveat

Evidence is substantially stronger than for oral phenylephrine.

## Phenylephrine (oral)

**Class:** Sympathomimetic decongestant\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Nasal congestion

### Evidence summary

High-quality clinical evidence has not established meaningful efficacy
at current OTC oral doses; FDA advisory review concluded evidence did
not support effectiveness.

### Cons / safety limitations

Systemic sympathomimetic adverse effects remain possible.

### Product/formulation caveat

Do not treat pharmacist recommendation share as proof of efficacy.

## Oxymetazoline

**Class:** Topical alpha-adrenergic decongestant\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Nasal congestion

### Evidence summary

Randomized trials support rapid, temporary congestion relief.

### Cons / safety limitations

Rebound congestion/rhinitis medicamentosa with prolonged use; typically
limited to a few days.

### Product/formulation caveat

Separate nasal spray efficacy from oral decongestant evidence.

## Dextromethorphan

**Class:** Antitussive\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Acute cough

### Evidence summary

Trials and systematic reviews show modest/inconsistent suppression of
acute cough; benefit varies by cause and population.

### Cons / safety limitations

Drowsiness, nausea and drug interactions; serotonergic toxicity risk
with some medicines; abuse risk at high doses.

### Product/formulation caveat

A cough suppressant treats the symptom, not the underlying infection.

## Guaifenesin

**Class:** Expectorant\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Productive/chesty cough

### Evidence summary

Randomized trials provide evidence for increased mucus clearance and
some symptom improvement, but effects are generally modest.

### Cons / safety limitations

GI upset, nausea, dizziness can occur.

### Product/formulation caveat

Evidence applies to guaifenesin, not automatically to multi-ingredient
products.

## Second-generation antihistamines

**Class:** Antihistamines\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Allergic rhinitis symptoms

### Evidence summary

Strong evidence for reducing sneezing, itching and rhinorrhea in
allergic rhinitis.

### Cons / safety limitations

Generally less sedating than first-generation agents, but individual
sedation can occur.

### Product/formulation caveat

These do not treat viral influenza itself.

## First-generation antihistamines

**Class:** Antihistamines\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Allergy; some cold symptoms

### Evidence summary

Evidence supports antihistamine effects on rhinorrhea in some cold
combinations, but sedation is a major tradeoff.

### Cons / safety limitations

Drowsiness, anticholinergic effects, impaired driving; greater concern
in older adults.

### Product/formulation caveat

Combination-product evidence should be separated from single-agent
evidence.

## Intranasal corticosteroids

**Class:** Anti-inflammatory nasal steroids\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Allergic rhinitis

### Evidence summary

Very strong evidence for nasal allergy symptom control, particularly
congestion.

### Cons / safety limitations

Nasal irritation/epistaxis; technique matters.

### Product/formulation caveat

Examples in the guide include fluticasone, triamcinolone and mometasone
brands.

## Zinc

**Class:** Mineral / lozenge\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Common-cold duration/severity

### Evidence summary

Meta-analyses support a possible reduction in cold duration when certain
zinc formulations/doses are started early, but results depend heavily on
salt, dose and formulation.

### Cons / safety limitations

Nausea/bad taste; intranasal zinc products have been associated with
loss of smell and are not equivalent to oral lozenges.

### Product/formulation caveat

Do not extrapolate from one zinc formulation to another.

## Famotidine

**Class:** H2 receptor antagonist\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Heartburn/acid-related symptoms

### Evidence summary

Strong evidence for acid suppression and relief of frequent heartburn;
less potent than PPIs for healing erosive disease.

### Cons / safety limitations

Headache/GI effects; renal dosing considerations.

### Product/formulation caveat

Current Zantac 360° products use famotidine; older ranitidine evidence
is not interchangeable.

## Omeprazole

**Class:** Proton-pump inhibitor\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Frequent heartburn/GERD symptoms

### Evidence summary

Strong randomized evidence for acid suppression and healing of erosive
esophagitis; OTC indication is short-term frequent heartburn.

### Cons / safety limitations

Headache/GI effects; long-term use has important monitoring/interaction
considerations.

### Product/formulation caveat

OTC studies generally evaluate defined courses, not indefinite daily
use.

## Loperamide

**Class:** Antidiarrheal\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Acute noninfectious diarrhea

### Evidence summary

Strong evidence for reducing stool frequency and improving stool
consistency in acute diarrhea.

### Cons / safety limitations

Constipation; dangerous cardiac/CNS toxicity at excessive doses; avoid
when invasive/inflammatory diarrhea is suspected.

### Product/formulation caveat

The 2026 guide lists Imodium at 81% in antidiarrheals.

## Bismuth subsalicylate

**Class:** Antidiarrheal/antacid\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Diarrhea, upset stomach, heartburn

### Evidence summary

Clinical trials support symptomatic treatment of acute diarrhea and
dyspepsia; also has antimicrobial activity against some organisms.

### Cons / safety limitations

Salicylate-related bleeding/interactions; tinnitus/salicylism with
excess; avoid in children/teens with viral illness.

### Product/formulation caveat

Pepto-Bismol/Kaopectate-type evidence should be separated from
loperamide evidence.

## Polyethylene glycol 3350

**Class:** Osmotic laxative\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Constipation

### Evidence summary

Strong evidence for increasing bowel movements and improving stool
consistency in chronic constipation.

### Cons / safety limitations

Bloating, diarrhea, cramping; prolonged unexplained constipation needs
evaluation.

### Product/formulation caveat

Evidence is stronger than for many stimulant/herbal laxatives.

## Bisacodyl

**Class:** Stimulant laxative\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Constipation

### Evidence summary

Controlled evidence supports short-term bowel-movement induction.

### Cons / safety limitations

Cramping, diarrhea; inappropriate chronic self-treatment can mask
underlying disease.

### Product/formulation caveat

Use product-specific dosing and duration.

## Lactase

**Class:** Digestive enzyme\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Lactose intolerance

### Evidence summary

Controlled studies support reducing lactose-related symptoms when taken
with lactose-containing foods.

### Cons / safety limitations

Generally well tolerated; effectiveness depends on dose and lactose
load.

### Product/formulation caveat

Evidence applies to lactase enzyme preparations.

## Melatonin

**Class:** Hormone supplement\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Sleep-onset problems / circadian
disorders

### Evidence summary

Meta-analyses show modest improvements in sleep-onset latency and
circadian timing in selected populations; effect is not equivalent to a
strong sedative.

### Cons / safety limitations

Daytime sleepiness, vivid dreams, variable supplement quality.

### Product/formulation caveat

Do not equate OTC supplement evidence with FDA-approved drug evidence.

## Benzoyl peroxide

**Class:** Topical antimicrobial/keratolytic\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Acne

### Evidence summary

Strong clinical evidence for inflammatory acne; often used alone or with
topical retinoids/antibiotics.

### Cons / safety limitations

Irritation, dryness, bleaching of fabrics.

### Product/formulation caveat

Exact concentration and combination matter.

## Salicylic acid

**Class:** Keratolytic\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Acne, warts, hyperkeratotic conditions

### Evidence summary

Evidence supports topical keratolysis; acne benefit is generally less
robust than benzoyl peroxide/retinoids.

### Cons / safety limitations

Irritation; excessive application can cause systemic salicylate exposure
in susceptible situations.

### Product/formulation caveat

Product concentration and body surface area matter.

## Hydrocortisone

**Class:** Topical corticosteroid\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Itch/inflammation from dermatitis and
bites

### Evidence summary

Strong evidence for short-term relief of inflammatory/pruritic
dermatoses.

### Cons / safety limitations

Skin atrophy with prolonged/excessive use; avoid inappropriate use on
infection or face/genitals without guidance.

### Product/formulation caveat

Topical steroid evidence does not support homeopathic topical analgesic
claims.

## Clotrimazole

**Class:** Topical azole antifungal\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Tinea/jock itch/athlete's foot

### Evidence summary

Strong randomized evidence for superficial dermatophyte infections.

### Cons / safety limitations

Local irritation/contact dermatitis.

### Product/formulation caveat

Treatment success depends on adequate duration and diagnosis.

## Terbinafine

**Class:** Topical allylamine antifungal\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Tinea pedis/cruris/corporis

### Evidence summary

High-quality trials show high mycologic and clinical cure rates, often
with shorter courses than some azoles.

### Cons / safety limitations

Local irritation; diagnosis must be correct.

### Product/formulation caveat

Among topical antifungals, terbinafine has particularly strong evidence
for dermatophytes.

## Sunscreen UV filters

**Class:** Photoprotective agents\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Sunburn prevention / UV protection

### Evidence summary

Controlled human studies and long-term observational evidence support
broad-spectrum sunscreen for reducing sunburn and cumulative UV damage;
randomized evidence for skin-cancer prevention is strongest for regular
use programs rather than individual brands.

### Cons / safety limitations

Irritation/contact allergy can occur; proper amount and reapplication
are critical.

### Product/formulation caveat

Brand recommendation percentage is not a measure of SPF efficacy.

## Loperamide + bismuth

**Class:** Antidiarrheal class\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Acute diarrhea

### Evidence summary

Both have clinical evidence, but they act differently and are not
interchangeable.

### Cons / safety limitations

Different contraindications and interaction profiles.

### Product/formulation caveat

Use exact active ingredient rather than brand name when evaluating
research.

## Calcium

**Class:** Mineral supplement\
**Relevant symptoms/conditions:** Bone health / calcium intake

### Evidence summary

Evidence supports correction of inadequate calcium intake;
fracture-prevention effects depend on population, baseline intake and
vitamin D status.

### Cons / safety limitations

Constipation, kidney-stone considerations in some populations; excessive
intake is not automatically beneficial.

### Product/formulation caveat

Tums appearing in the calcium category reflects calcium carbonate
content, not an indication that antacid use is equivalent to
osteoporosis therapy.

# Part IV --- Detailed evidence already established for the homeopathic-flu products

## Sambucol / black elderberry

The clinical literature primarily studies **Sambucus nigra**
preparations rather than every current Sambucol formulation.

### 1995 randomized influenza-B study

Zakay-Rones et al. reported a very large apparent effect during an
influenza-B outbreak. The paper reported symptom improvement in
**93.3%** of the elderberry group within 2 days versus **91.7%** of
controls within 6 days, and reported approximately 90% complete cure
within 2--3 days in the treated group.

This is an unusually strong early result from a small study and should
not be treated as a universal modern "90% cure rate."

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9395631/

### 2004 randomized influenza A/B study

A randomized, double-blind trial of 60 adults reported symptoms relieved
approximately **4 days earlier** in the elderberry group and lower
rescue-medication use.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15080016/

### 2016 randomized traveler/common-cold study

Among 312 air travelers, the trial did not establish prevention of
catching a cold, but among cold episodes it reported fewer total
cold-episode days and lower symptom scores with elderberry.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27023596/

### 2019 meta-analysis

A meta-analysis of randomized trials reported a large overall effect on
upper-respiratory symptoms, but the total evidence base was only 180
participants.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30670267/

### 2020 randomized influenza trial

Macknin et al. randomized 87 patients with influenza. Time to all
symptoms being none/mild was **4.9 days placebo vs 5.3 days elderberry
(p=0.57)**. Complete symptom resolution was **8.7 vs 8.6 days
(p=0.87)**.

This is the most important negative trial against simple claims that
elderberry reliably shortens influenza.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32929634/

### 2021 systematic review

A systematic review of five randomized trials concluded that elderberry
**may** reduce cold/influenza duration or severity, but certainty was
low/uncertain.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33827515/

**Overall:** real human RCT evidence exists, but results conflict. A
single success percentage is not defensible.

# Boiron Oscillococcinum

## Cochrane review, 2015

The latest major Cochrane review included **6 studies**: - 2 prevention
studies, 327 participants - 4 treatment studies, 1,196 participants

# Part VI --- Research-quality rules for the full project

A product will receive a **High / Moderate / Low / Very Low / No
product-specific evidence** rating.

### High

Large, well-designed randomized trials and/or high-quality systematic
reviews directly studying the relevant formulation.

### Moderate

Several RCTs with some limitations, or strong ingredient evidence but
imperfect formulation matching.

### Low

Small trials, heterogeneous formulations, inconsistent outcomes, or
substantial risk of bias.

### Very Low

Case series, uncontrolled studies, laboratory studies, or highly
indirect evidence.

### No product-specific evidence

No adequate controlled clinical trial of the exact commercial product
was located.

# Part IX --- Expanded study-level evidence extraction

## Important limitation on "dump entire study data"

This dossier is intended for downstream AI processing, so the preferred
representation is **structured study data** rather than reproducing full
journal articles. Full articles are copyrighted and cannot be copied
wholesale into this file. The file therefore captures the clinically
important study data that can be extracted from the available records:
population, intervention, comparator, dose, duration, outcomes, effect
estimates, confidence intervals, p-values, adverse events, study
limitations, funding/affiliation signals when reported, and source
identifiers.

Where a PubMed abstract or systematic-review record exposes only part of
the numerical dataset, the missing values are explicitly marked **not
reported in the accessible record** rather than guessed.

## PSEUDOEPHEDRINE

### Study: Taverner, Danz & Economos, 1999

**Citation:** *The effects of oral pseudoephedrine on nasal patency in
the common cold: a double-blind single-dose placebo-controlled trial.*
PMID 10196648. DOI 10.1046/j.1365-2273.1999.00208.x.

**Design** - Double-blind randomized placebo-controlled clinical
trial. - 54 previously healthy people with common cold. - 52
completed. - Symptoms present ≤5 days. - Moderate-to-severe nasal
congestion.

**Intervention** - Pseudoephedrine 60 mg single oral dose.

**Comparator** - Placebo.

**Measurements** - Subjective congestion. - Acoustic rhinometry. - Nasal
minimum cross-sectional area. - Nasal volume. - Active posterior
rhinomanometry.

**Results** - Subjective congestion improved significantly at 60, 90,
120 and 150 minutes after pseudoephedrine. - Total nasal minimum
cross-sectional area increased significantly versus placebo:
**P=0.018**. - Nasal volume increased significantly versus placebo:
**P=0.003**. - Active posterior rhinomanometry did not show a
significant difference.

**Interpretation** - Provides direct evidence that a 60-mg oral dose can
temporarily improve congestion in adults with the common cold. -
Objective and subjective measures were not uniformly positive.

**Source** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10196648/

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Study: Jawad & Eccles, 1998

**Citation:** *Effect of pseudoephedrine on nasal airflow in patients
with nasal congestion associated with common cold.* PMID 9695162.

**Design** - Randomized controlled trial. - 40 participants. -
Pseudoephedrine n=20; placebo n=20. - Two doses separated by 4 hours.

**Intervention** - Pseudoephedrine 60 mg.

**Outcome** - Unilateral nasal airflow measured over 7 hours.

**Result** - The study found an objective nasal-airflow benefit with
pseudoephedrine.

**Limitation** - Small sample. - Objective airflow endpoint does not
automatically equal meaningful improvement in all cold symptoms.

**Source** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9695162/

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Study: Latte et al., 2004

**Citation:** *A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of
pseudoephedrine in coryza.* PMID 15236629. DOI
10.1111/j.1440-1681.2004.04013.x.

**Design** - Double-blind randomized placebo-controlled. - 48 adults
with acute coryza. - Single 60-mg dose.

**Results** - Nasal airway resistance decreased significantly: -
**P=0.005** - 95% CI **0.073 to 0.383** for the reported treatment
effect. - Nasal volume increased but did not reach statistical
significance: - **P=0.07** - No significant change in minimum
cross-sectional area. - No significant change in subjective
nasal-congestion symptom score.

**Interpretation** - Moderate objective decongestant effect. - Important
counterexample to overclaiming subjective benefit from every
pseudoephedrine trial.

**Source** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15236629/

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Study: Eccles et al., 2005

**Citation:** *Efficacy and safety of single and multiple doses of
pseudoephedrine in the treatment of nasal congestion associated with
common cold.* PMID 15794071.

**Design** - Double-blind randomized parallel-group
placebo-controlled. - **238** patients with acute upper respiratory
tract infection. - Mean age approximately 20 years. - Single-dose day 1
and repeated dosing through day 3.

**Intervention** - Pseudoephedrine 60 mg.

**Primary objective outcome** - Area under the nasal-airway-resistance
curve.

**Results** - Day 1 NAR AUC 0--3 h: significantly lower with
pseudoephedrine, **P=0.006**. - Day 1 NAR AUC 0--4 h: **P=0.001**. - Day
3 NAR AUC 0--3 h and 0--4 h: both **P\<0.001**. - Day 1 subjective
congestion VAS AUC 0--3 h: **P=0.029**. - Day 1 subjective congestion
VAS AUC 0--4 h: **P=0.021**. - Day 3 subjective differences were not
significant. - Overall congestion/stuffiness summary score over the
study was better with pseudoephedrine, **P=0.016**.

**Interpretation** - Stronger support for short-term decongestion than
many other oral decongestant studies. - Repeated dosing may maintain
objective benefit while subjective differences can diminish.

**Source** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15794071/

### Study: Gelotte et al., 2019 --- children

**Citation:** *A Multicenter, Randomized, Placebo-Controlled Study of
Pseudoephedrine for the Temporary Relief of Nasal Congestion in Children
With the Common Cold.* PMID 31274197. DOI 10.1002/jcph.1472.

**Population** - Children aged 6--11 years. - Multicenter double-blind
placebo-controlled randomized trial.

**Intervention** - Pseudoephedrine HCl 30 mg tablets. - Current OTC
monograph dose. - Up to 7 days as needed.

**Primary endpoint** - Weighted change from baseline in instantaneous
nasal-congestion severity over 1--8 hours after first dose.

**Result** - Pseudoephedrine was superior to placebo. - Least-squares
mean treatment difference: **1.2**. - **P=0.029**.

**Safety** - Somnolence: - pseudoephedrine **71.9%** - placebo
**63.9%** - Insomnia: - pseudoephedrine **34.4%** - placebo **38.9%** -
Nervousness: - pseudoephedrine **20.0%** - placebo **23.6%**

**Interpretation** - Demonstrates short-term pediatric decongestant
efficacy at the studied age/dose. - The high reported somnolence
percentages demonstrate why adverse-event definitions and baseline
symptoms matter.

**Source** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31274197/

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Study: Latte & Taverner, 2007

**Citation:** *Clinical trial of 3 days of treatment with oral
pseudoephedrine for the common cold in the southern hemisphere.* PMID
17882915. DOI 10.2500/ajr.2007.21.3041.

**Population** - **216** subjects. - Double-blind randomized
placebo-controlled. - Three days.

**Intervention** - Pseudoephedrine 60 mg four times daily.

**Results** - After first dose, small improvements across objective
congestion measures. - Prespecified primary endpoint (day-1 NAR AUC) was
**not significantly different**. - Total nasal volume increased
**5.5%**, **P=0.031**, during 0.5--3 h. - Pooled day 1/day 3 VAS
decrease was **7.0%**, **P=0.072**.

**Interpretation** - Important negative/weak result. - Shows that
pseudoephedrine's objective vasoconstrictor effect does not always
translate into a statistically significant symptom benefit.

**Source** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17882915/

### Pseudoephedrine evidence synthesis

**Best-supported benefit** - Short-term nasal congestion relief. -
Strongest evidence is for objective nasal patency and short-term symptom
improvement.

**Not established** - Meaningful shortening of the underlying viral
illness. - Prevention of colds.

**Important adverse effects** - Insomnia. - Nervousness. - Somnolence in
some pediatric studies. - Cardiovascular stimulation can be clinically
relevant.

**Evidence grade** **Moderate-to-high for short-term nasal
decongestion**, depending on population and endpoint.

# ACETAMINOPHEN FOR COMMON-COLD SYMPTOMS

## Cochrane review

**Citation:** *Acetaminophen (paracetamol) for the common cold in
adults.* PMID 23818046.

### Evidence base

-   **4 RCTs**
-   **758 participants**.

The review did not pool results because of substantial differences in
designs, outcomes and time points.

### Findings

Nasal obstruction: - Significant improvement in 2/4 studies.

Rhinorrhea: - One study found acetaminophen superior to placebo.

No convincing improvement: - sore throat; - malaise; - sneezing; -
cough.

Headache/aches: - 2 studies favored acetaminophen. - 1 study found no
difference.

Duration: - None of the included studies reported common-cold duration.

Adverse effects: - GI events; - dizziness; - dry mouth; - somnolence; -
increased sweating.

### Conclusion

Acetaminophen may relieve some cold symptoms, particularly nasal
obstruction/rhinorrhea and headache/aches, but the evidence base is
sparse and does not establish shortening of the illness.

**Source** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23818046/

# COMBINATION COLD PRODUCTS

## Pseudoephedrine + acetaminophen

**Study:** randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial.

**N:** **430** participants: - active n=216; - placebo n=214.

**Population** - Cold symptoms ≤48 hours. - At least moderate overall
"sinus" symptoms.

**Results** Two hours after second dose: - overall sinus symptom score
reduction: - active **−1.30 ± 0.06** - placebo **−0.93 ± 0.06** -
**P≤0.029** - weighted sinus pressure/pain/congestion: - active **−1.14
± 0.06** - placebo **−0.84 ± 0.06** - **P≤0.029**

**Source** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11115196/

### Interpretation

This supports the combination for short-term symptom relief of
congestion/pressure/pain.

It does not prove: - antiviral activity; - shortened illness; -
prevention; - superiority over each component for every symptom.

## Aspirin + pseudoephedrine

**Study:** multicenter randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial.

**N:** **643** patients with acute URTI.

**Interventions** - ASA 1000 mg + pseudoephedrine 60 mg. - ASA 500 mg +
pseudoephedrine 30 mg. - Paracetamol 1000 mg + pseudoephedrine 60 mg. -
Placebo.

**Primary endpoint** - AUC of nasal-congestion score during first 2
hours.

**Result** - All active treatments were statistically superior to
placebo on the primary endpoint. - Benefit remained significant for up
to 6 hours. - Lower-dose ASA/pseudoephedrine did not show significant
benefit in some subgroup analyses, particularly when baseline congestion
was severe or moderate depending on the comparison.

**Source** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15500197/

### Interpretation

Combination evidence can be strong for a defined symptom endpoint while
still not proving that the whole multi-symptom product shortens a viral
illness.

# Part X --- Evidence hierarchy for the family-reference version

When the same product has many papers, the dossier should prioritize
evidence in this order:

1.  **Latest high-quality systematic review/meta-analysis**
2.  **Cochrane review**
3.  Large preregistered randomized controlled trial
4.  Multiple independent RCTs
5.  Smaller RCT
6.  Prospective cohort
7.  Retrospective observational study
8.  Case-control study
9.  Case series/report
10. Mechanistic/laboratory study
11. Animal study
12. Manufacturer/marketing material

A lower-level study should never override a well-conducted higher-level
study merely because its result looks more dramatic.

# Part XII --- Current research-status corrections

## Correction 1: "OTC drug" is not synonymous with "proven effective"

The Pharmacy Times guide is a **pharmacist recommendation survey**. It
is useful for identifying products pharmacists commonly recommend, but
it is not itself an efficacy evidence hierarchy. Pharmacy Times states
that the 2026 guide is based on a national survey of **1,700 practicing
pharmacists** covering **145 product categories**.
citeturn0search3turn0search0

## Correction 2: 145 categories does not mean 145 unique drugs

The same brand can appear in several categories, and the same active
ingredient can appear under many brands.

Examples: - Mucinex → guaifenesin, sometimes + dextromethorphan. - Advil
→ ibuprofen. - Tylenol → acetaminophen. - Sudafed → pseudoephedrine or
phenylephrine depending on formulation. - Zicam → multiple
formulations/ingredients. - Flonase → fluticasone propionate. - Pepcid →
famotidine. - Imodium → loperamide.

Therefore the master dossier must eventually **deduplicate at the
active-ingredient/formulation level**.

## Correction 3: Formulation-specific evidence is essential

The same brand name can encompass: - tablets; - liquid; -
extended-release tablets; - nasal sprays; - lozenges; - gummies; -
combination products.

A study of one cannot automatically validate another.

## Correction 4: Homeopathic products require separate treatment

Homeopathic products should not be grouped with pharmacologically active
herbal extracts.

Examples: - Oscillococcinum: highly diluted homeopathic preparation. -
Pelargonium/EPs 7630: pharmacologically active herbal extract. -
Elderberry: botanical extract with variable composition.

These should receive separate evidence grades.

# Part XIV --- Family-use safety layer

Because this document is intended for family reference, **efficacy
evidence must never be separated from triage/safety**.

For each product, the final dossier should include:

### "Do not self-treat / seek medical evaluation" triggers

Examples: - difficulty breathing; - chest pain; - severe dehydration; -
confusion; - fainting; - severe allergic reaction; - blood in
stool/vomit; - black/tarry stool; - persistent high fever; - severe or
rapidly worsening pain; - symptoms lasting substantially longer than
expected; - suspected overdose; - accidental pediatric ingestion; -
pregnancy-specific concerns; - major kidney/liver disease; - significant
drug interactions.

### Special-population flags

Every final drug entry should explicitly identify whether evidence
differs for: - children; - older adults; - pregnancy; - breastfeeding; -
renal impairment; - hepatic impairment; - hypertension/cardiovascular
disease; - diabetes; - anticoagulant use; - seizure disorders; -
glaucoma; - urinary retention/prostate disease.

This is particularly important for: - pseudoephedrine; -
first-generation antihistamines; - NSAIDs; - acetaminophen; -
loperamide; - salicylates; - sedating cough medicines.
