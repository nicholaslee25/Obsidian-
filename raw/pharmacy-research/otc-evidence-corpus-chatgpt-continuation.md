## Revised research standard (stated methodology going forward)

For every individual active ingredient/product, capture:

Generic/active ingredient; Brand(s) appearing in the Pharmacy Times category; Exact OTC indication; Legitimate symptoms/conditions treated; Mechanism; FDA status where applicable; Typical dose; Maximum OTC dose; Duration; Age restrictions; Route/formulation; RCTs; Meta-analyses; Systematic reviews; Cochrane reviews; Large observational studies; Pediatric studies; Elderly studies; Pregnancy data where available; Head-to-head comparisons; Placebo comparisons; Absolute response rate; Relative risk; Odds ratio; Hazard ratio where relevant; Mean difference/SMD; Confidence intervals; p-values; NNT/NNH when calculable; Time to benefit; Duration of benefit; Adverse effects; Serious adverse effects; Discontinuation rates; Drug interactions; Disease interactions; Contraindications; Overdose/toxicity information; Rebound/tolerance/dependence evidence; Cost-effectiveness studies; Quality-of-life outcomes; Study limitations; Publication bias; Heterogeneity; Industry sponsorship; Conflicts of interest; Exact citation; PMID; DOI; Journal; Year; Study population; Sample size; Study design.

**Core rule:** explicitly distinguish "pharmacist recommended" from "clinically effective." Pharmacy Times numbers are survey/recommendation data (e.g. Imodium 81% of antidiarrheal recommendations, Pepcid 83% of H2-blocker recommendations), not efficacy/response percentages.

**Supplements get harder scrutiny:** for vitamin D, vitamin C, magnesium, CoQ10, omega-3, cranberry, probiotics, garlic, turmeric, glucosamine/chondroitin, echinacea, elderberry, melatonin, etc. — deliberately capture positive evidence, negative evidence, and evidence of no benefit. "No convincing evidence of benefit" is treated as equally important data as "evidence of benefit."

**EENT/Diagnostics not skipped:** artificial tears, dry-eye products, ocular antihistamines, redness relievers, earwax removal, nasal saline, nasal corticosteroids/antihistamines/decongestants, thermometers, glucose meters, lancets, COVID tests, pregnancy tests, oral-care diagnostics — treated differently from drugs. Diagnostic products get: sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, likelihood ratios, false-positive/negative rate, limit of detection, agreement with reference standard, time to result, usability, failure rate, population tested, symptomatic vs. asymptomatic performance, variant/strain effects, regulatory status — explicitly NOT drug-style efficacy percentages.

## OTC EVIDENCE CORPUS — SUPPLEMENTAL VOLUME: EENT + DIAGNOSTICS + VITAMINS/SUPPLEMENTS

### Source of scope
The 2026 Pharmacy Times OTC Guide contains 145 product categories surveyed from ~1,700 practicing pharmacists, spanning EENT, Diagnostics, Vitamins & Dietary Supplements, Diabetes Healthcare, Durable Goods, Oral Care, Pediatrics, Women's Health, Gastrointestinal, Topicals, First Aid, Pain and Inflammation, and Cough/Cold/Allergy. Note: category composition has changed over the years (older guides had 149); this corpus uses the current 2026-27 guide as scope.

### PART I — EENT

**1. Artificial Tears / Ophthalmic Lubricants**
Primarily symptomatic therapy for dry-eye disease (DED) and ocular-surface discomfort; does not generally cure the underlying cause. Common OTC ingredients: carboxymethylcellulose, hypromellose, glycerin, polyethylene glycol, propylene glycol, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, povidone, mineral-oil-based formulations, lipid-containing emulsions, preservative-free formulations. Evidence is heavily formulation-dependent — a low-viscosity aqueous lubricant, a lipid-containing emulsion, and a preservative-free formulation have different pharmacologic/physical properties. Entries should preserve: active ingredient → concentration → formulation → preservative → dosing frequency.

*2026 Cochrane review (context, not itself OTC):* Priyadarshini SR, et al. Topical cyclosporine A therapy for dry eye disease. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2026. PMID: 42464966. Search date March 17, 2026. RCTs comparing topical cyclosporine vs. vehicle/artificial tears/alternative concentrations. Demonstrates DED is not simply "lack of water" — ocular-surface inflammation is a major component. Database interpretation: artificial tears = lubricating/symptomatic treatment, not disease-modifying treatment for all dry-eye disease.

**2. Ear Wax Removal**
Common OTC approaches: carbamide peroxide, hydrogen peroxide, mineral oil, saline, glycerol, sodium bicarbonate, proprietary cerumenolytics.

*Cochrane review — ear drops:* Aaron K, et al. Ear drops for the removal of ear wax. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2018. PMID: 30043448. RCTs in adults/children with obstructing/impacted cerumen. Outcomes: complete clearance, adverse effects (primary); amount removed, symptom relief, need for further intervention, mechanical-removal success, cost (secondary). Searched Cochrane ENT Trials Register, CENTRAL, PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science, ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP. **Result: evidence did not establish a universally superior ear-drop formulation** — a claim like "Product X dissolves earwax better than everything else" is not supported.

*Network meta-analysis of cerumenolytics:* PMID: 33326697, DOI 10.1111/coa.13692. 26 studies in systematic review, 25 in network meta-analysis. Compared sodium bicarbonate, paradichlorobenzene, chlorobutanol, glycerol, docusate sodium, hydrogen peroxide, oils, urea/hydrogen peroxide/glycerol, combinations. Vs. normal saline: sodium bicarbonate OR 2.68 (95% CI 1.2–6.1); paradichlorobenzene OR 30.9 (95% CI 5.9–161.3, ranked highest). Other agents (chlorobutanol, glycerol, docusate sodium, hydrogen peroxide, oil, combinations) showed significant effects in particular circumstances. Caveat: many treatments were evaluated alongside syringing/aspiration/suction, so drops alone may not independently "remove" wax. Correct framing: "Cerumenolytics may soften/disperse impacted wax and improve subsequent mechanical removal; evidence varies considerably by agent and protocol" — not "earwax drops dissolve all earwax."

**3. Sore Throat Lozenges**

*3.1 Amylmetacresol + 2,4-dichlorobenzyl alcohol (AMC/DCBA, e.g. Strepsils):*
Weckmann G, Hauptmann-Voß A, Baumeister SE, Klötzer C, Chenot JF. Efficacy of AMC/DCBA lozenges for sore throat: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Int J Clin Pract. 2017. PMID: 28869700, DOI 10.1111/ijcp.13002. 77 citations screened, 3 RCTs met inclusion (660 adults, acute uncomplicated sore throat). Formulation: amylmetacresol 0.6 mg + 2,4-dichlorobenzyl alcohol 1.2 mg. Primary endpoint: pain intensity ~2 hours post-treatment. Result: SMD −0.60 (95% CI −0.75 to −0.45) on an 11-point pain scale, favoring active lozenge. Safety: no serious AEs; mild effects (headache, cough, nasal congestion, oral irritation) in up to ~16% of subjects in both groups. **Major limitation: all included trials were manufacturer-sponsored.**

*Large RCT:* McNally D, et al. Rapid relief of acute sore throat with AMC/DCBA throat lozenges: RCT. PMID: 19849767, DOI 10.1111/j.1742-1241.2009.02230.x. N=310 (155 active/155 placebo). Pain reduction detectable at 5 minutes, persisting ~2 hours. Also improved sore-throat relief, swallowing difficulty, functional impairment. No AE difference between groups.

*AMC/DCBA + lidocaine RCT:* PMID: 22579007. N=190 randomized to AMC/DCBA+lidocaine, hexylresorcinol, or placebo. AMC/DCBA+lidocaine onset ~1–10 min; hexylresorcinol onset ~1–5 min. **AMC/DCBA+lidocaine did NOT reach significance on its primary endpoint**, though several secondary endpoints favored active treatment — kept as an example of a partially negative trial.

**4. Flurbiprofen lozenges** (NSAID, not just demulcent)

Schachtel BP, et al. Flurbiprofen 8.75 mg lozenges for treating sore throat symptoms: RCT. PMID: 27734772, DOI 10.2217/pmt-2015-0001. N=204 (102/102). Dosing: up to 5 lozenges every 3–6h for up to 7 days. First 24h vs. placebo: sore-throat pain 47% greater reduction; difficulty swallowing 66% greater reduction; swollen throat 40% greater reduction (all P<0.05).

Second RCT: PMID: 27517548. N=320 (8.75mg n=128, 12.5mg n=64, placebo n=128). 8.75mg significantly outperformed placebo for total pain relief, throat soreness, swollen throat, detectable within 2 hours.

Distinction: AMC/DCBA → local symptomatic analgesia; flurbiprofen → local NSAID-mediated anti-inflammatory/analgesic action. Neither treats the underlying viral infection.

### PART — DIAGNOSTICS (evidence-schema note, not brand-specific data)

Categories: at-home COVID-19 tests, blood glucose monitors, blood sampling devices/lancets, digital thermometers. Evidence fields differ completely from drugs: reference standard, sample type, population, symptomatic/asymptomatic, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, LR+, LR-, false-positive/negative rate, limit of detection, time to result, invalid-result rate, operator training, environment, device version, manufacturer, study date, variant/strain.

**COVID tests:** performance depends on variant, viral load, symptom timing, vaccination status, specimen type, test generation, comparator PCR assay — an old sensitivity estimate should never be applied to a current test. Store as "Test X: sensitivity X%, specificity Y%, population Z, vs. reference standard Q, period D" — not a blanket "rapid tests are 80% accurate."

**Glucose meters:** accuracy is not one number — relevant standards: ISO 15197, FDA expectations, lab-reference comparison. Outcomes: mean absolute relative difference, % readings within ±15 mg/dL (glucose <100) or ±15% (glucose ≥100), error-grid analysis, clinically significant error, strip lot variation, hematocrit effects, interfering substances (acetaminophen, vitamin C, temperature/humidity, expired strips, insufficient sample).

**Lancets:** not drugs — evidence concerns pain, puncture depth, bleeding, tissue trauma, infection, user preference, reuse practices, gauge, spring mechanism, sampling location. Major safety issue is usually reuse/shared-use infection control, not the device itself.

**Digital thermometers:** agreement with reference thermometer; oral/axillary/rectal/tympanic/temporal site; a stated resolution (e.g. 0.1°F) is not the same as accuracy of ±0.1°F.

### PART II — VITAMINS & DIETARY SUPPLEMENTS

Core methodological point: a supplement can be biologically active without producing a clinically meaningful benefit. Every supplement needs: biochemical effect, surrogate endpoint, patient-important endpoint, evidence in deficient vs. non-deficient populations, dose, duration, formulation, safety, interactions.

**Vitamin C:** general population — routine supplementation does not reliably prevent common colds; regular (not after-onset) supplementation can modestly reduce cold duration/severity, more apparent under extreme physical/cold stress. Starting after symptoms begin has not shown large consistent benefit. 2026 Pharmacy Times context: liposomal vitamin C improves PK/PD measures; translation to meaningful immune benefit remains inconclusive (improved absorption ≠ proven improved clinical outcomes).

**Vitamin D:** must distinguish deficiency treatment from supplementation in already-replete people — different evidence questions. Deficiency treatment: clearly important. High-dose supplementation in replete people: does NOT establish prevention of cancer, cardiovascular disease, infection, depression, fractures generally, or mortality. 2026 example: phase 3 SOLARIS trial, high-dose vitamin D3 + first-line chemo in metastatic colorectal cancer — safe, but no significant PFS improvement. Kept explicitly as a negative study so the model doesn't learn "vitamin D is good for cancer."

**Calcium:** two separate evidence questions — correcting inadequate intake vs. supplementing already-sufficient intake. Outcomes: bone mineral density, osteopenia, fractures, colorectal outcomes, cardiovascular outcomes, kidney stones. An entry must specify baseline dietary intake, menopausal status, vitamin D status, dose, duration, fracture endpoint, AEs — not just "calcium strengthens bones."

**Magnesium:** highly indication-specific — deficiency, migraine, constipation, pregnancy-related conditions, blood pressure, sleep, muscle cramps are separate evidence questions (magnesium-as-laxative ≠ magnesium-for-deficiency ≠ proof it improves sleep).

**CoQ10:** marketed for cardiovascular support/energy/antioxidant/statin-associated muscle symptoms. Strongest research questions: heart failure, statin-associated muscle symptoms, migraine, BP/metabolic endpoints. Antioxidant activity is mechanistic, not a demonstrated patient benefit. Pharmacy Times 2026: Nature Made leads CoQ10 recommendation at 51% (recommendation share, not clinical response).

**Cranberry:** main clinically meaningful question is prevention of recurrent UTI — substantially different from "cleanses urinary tract," treats an active UTI, kills pathogens, or replaces antibiotics. Evidence is about prevention of recurrence, not treatment of established infection.

**Fiber:** better-supported because of direct physiological mechanism/measurable outcomes (stool frequency/consistency, constipation, LDL, glycemic response, satiety) — but fiber type matters (psyllium, methylcellulose, wheat dextrin, inulin, partially hydrolyzed guar gum are not identical). For constipation, soluble/bulking fibers can help meaningfully; adequate fluid + gradual dose escalation matter.

**Garlic:** studied for BP, cholesterol, cardiovascular risk, immune claims. Small effects on surrogate cardiovascular markers; evidence for major clinical outcomes much weaker. "Garlic lowers BP somewhat" ≠ "garlic prevents heart attacks" (needs cardiovascular-outcome trials).

**Omega-3/fish oil:** must distinguish EPA/DHA supplements, prescription purified EPA, and dietary fish consumption — not interchangeable. Endpoints: triglycerides, cardiovascular events, sudden cardiac death, stroke, inflammation, pregnancy/neurodevelopment, cognition. Large triglyceride reduction does not automatically mean fewer heart attacks (classic surrogate-endpoint trap).

**Glucosamine/chondroitin:** relevant condition is osteoarthritis. Outcomes to track separately: WOMAC pain, physical function, stiffness, rescue analgesic use, radiographic joint-space narrowing, surgery, quality of life. Symptom improvement ≠ structural disease modification.

**Melatonin:**
- 2024 dose/timing review, PMID 38888087: 26 RCTs, 1,689 observations. Dose-response modeling suggested increasing effects up to ~4 mg/day; timing ~3 hours before desired bedtime modeled as potentially useful. Does not establish 4mg as correct for everyone.
- Chronic insomnia systematic review/meta-analysis of 24 RCTs: in adults with non-comorbid chronic insomnia, statistically significant improvement NOT consistently demonstrated for sleep-onset latency, total sleep time, or sleep efficiency. Effects more apparent in pediatric/adolescent and some comorbid-insomnia populations. → "melatonin — age/indication dependent."

**Multivitamins:** Pharmacy Times 2026 survey: Centrum 33%, One A Day 27%, Nature Made 22%, Geritol 4%, OLLY 4%, vitafusion 3%, Nature's Way Alive! 3%, Theragran-M Advanced 3% (recommendation %, not efficacy %). Evidence questions are separate and complicated: nutrient-deficiency prevention (strong rationale) vs. chronic-disease prevention, mortality, cancer, cognitive decline (all require large long-duration trials) — "contains 100% DV" ≠ "prevents disease."

**Prenatal vitamins:** not one generic supplement — evidence is strongest for specific nutrients/outcomes: folic acid, iron, iodine, vitamin D (baseline-dependent), calcium (dietary-intake/risk-dependent). Distinguish preconception folic acid vs. pregnancy iron vs. general prenatal multivitamin use — different evidence bases/endpoints.

**Iron:** strong benefit when deficiency exists, potential harm when unnecessary. Evidence questions: hemoglobin response, ferritin, anemia correction, fatigue, pregnancy outcomes, GI AEs, constipation, adherence, oral vs. IV. Do not encode "iron gives you energy" — correct: "Iron supplementation corrects iron deficiency/iron-deficiency anemia; symptomatic benefit expected when deficiency is present."

**Probiotics:** Pharmacy Times 2026 coverage itself emphasizes matching strain/dose/shelf-life potency rather than treating "probiotics" as one intervention. A proper record needs: genus, species, strain, CFU dose, product, indication, population, duration, endpoint, comparator, storage conditions, viability at administration. "Lactobacillus" alone is not a sufficient identifier — evidence for one strain does not transfer to another strain of the same genus.

**Supplement database rule:** never generalize evidence from an ingredient to every formulation containing it. Examples: fish oil ≠ all omega-3 products; probiotics ≠ all probiotics; magnesium oxide ≠ magnesium glycinate; vitamin D2 ≠ vitamin D3 in every context; curcumin ≠ every turmeric supplement; CoQ10 ubiquinone ≠ every ubiquinol formulation; cranberry juice ≠ standardized cranberry extract; melatonin tablet ≠ every extended-release formulation.

**Two-layer rule (critical, applies to the whole corpus):**
- Layer A — Pharmacy Times recommendation data, e.g. "Nature Made — CoQ10 — 51%" = 51% of pharmacist survey recommendations.
- Layer B — Clinical evidence, e.g. "RCT/meta-analysis → N participants → dose → endpoint → effect."
These must never be merged into a single "score" — otherwise a downstream model could wrongly learn "pharmacists recommend it 51%, therefore it works 51% of the time."

## OTC EVIDENCE CORPUS VOLUME II — GASTROINTESTINAL, PAIN, COUGH/COLD/ALLERGY, FIRST AID, TOPICALS, DIABETES, ORAL CARE & WOMEN'S HEALTH

**Evidence hierarchy used:** Tier 1 = systematic reviews/meta-analyses/Cochrane/large RCTs. Tier 2 = smaller RCTs/controlled trials/prospective observational. Tier 3 = pharmacologic-mechanistic/retrospective/case series. Tier 4 = expert opinion/manufacturer info/pharmacist recommendation surveys. Reminder: Imodium 81% and Pepcid 83% are recommendation shares (n≈1700 pharmacists), not response rates.

### PART III — GASTROINTESTINAL

**Loperamide (antidiarrheal):** peripheral μ-opioid agonist in intestinal smooth muscle; decreases propulsive activity, increases transit time and water/electrolyte absorption; very limited CNS penetration at therapeutic doses. Strongly supported for reducing stool frequency in acute diarrhea (endpoint: time to last unformed stool / number of unformed stools). Treats symptom, not infectious cause — avoid in bloody diarrhea, high fever, suspected invasive bacterial dysentery, certain inflammatory diarrheas. Loperamide+simethicone combination: trials generally show faster relief of multiple acute-diarrhea symptoms than either alone (simethicone addressing the gas/bloating component specifically, not diarrhea itself). **Major safety issue:** massive supratherapeutic/abuse doses linked to serious cardiac toxicity — QT prolongation, torsades de pointes, ventricular arrhythmias, syncope, cardiac arrest. Store therapeutic-dose safety and abuse-dose toxicity as separate facts; do not let a model infer high-dose loperamide is safe because therapeutic-dose loperamide is safe.

**Famotidine (Pepcid, H2-receptor antagonist):** blocks histamine H2 receptors on gastric parietal cells → decreases acid secretion. OTC use: heartburn, acid indigestion, prevention of meal-associated heartburn, short-term acid symptoms. Extensive RCT evidence for acid suppression/heartburn improvement; useful for intermittent symptoms needing rapid-ish suppression without continuous PPI therapy. PPI > H2 blocker for stronger/more sustained suppression, but that doesn't make H2 blockers inferior for every patient. Tachyphylaxis: repeated exposure can reduce acid-suppressive effect, especially with continuous use (one reason PPIs dominate for frequent GERD).

**Antacids:** calcium carbonate (e.g. Tums) — inexpensive, rapid onset, high neutralizing capacity; downsides: constipation, belching, hypercalcemia with excess, acid rebound. Magnesium salts — effective, but diarrhea/laxative effect. Aluminum salts — more likely constipation. Combination Mg+Al products balance Mg-diarrhea against Al-constipation (useful pharmacologic principle).

**Proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole):** irreversibly inhibit gastric H+/K+-ATPase → much stronger acid suppression than antacids/H2 blockers. Among best-supported classes for erosive esophagitis/GERD; generally outperform H2 blockers for healing erosive esophagitis. Not designed for "take one whenever you feel heartburn" — for intermittent symptoms, antacids/H2 blockers may fit better; PPIs work best used per their pharmacology (before meals, consistently). Long-term safety controversy: observational studies associate prolonged use with kidney disease, fractures, C. diff, pneumonia, micronutrient deficiencies, dementia — but association ≠ causation, confounding by indication is a major problem, and randomized evidence is generally more reassuring than the worst observational associations. Do not encode "PPIs cause dementia" — correct framing keeps the uncertainty explicit.

**PEG 3350:** osmotic laxative, retains water in intestinal lumen. Among the strongest evidence bases of OTC constipation products — increased stool frequency, improved consistency, reduced straining vs. placebo. RCT example: PEG vs. tegaserod, N=237, treatment success 50.0% (PEG) vs. 30.8% (tegaserod), P=.003 — a genuine patient-level success rate, not a recommendation percentage.

**Bisacodyl (stimulant laxative):** stimulates intestinal motility/secretion; useful for short-term constipation/bowel evacuation. AEs: cramping, diarrhea, urgency. RCTs show increased bowel movements/improved constipation vs. placebo. The "stimulant laxatives cause lazy bowel with routine use" claim isn't well supported by evidence; the bigger concern is inappropriate chronic self-treatment masking an undiagnosed cause of persistent constipation.

**Senna:** sennosides stimulate colonic motility/secretion; evidence supports efficacy in constipation. Typical problems: abdominal cramping, diarrhea, electrolyte disturbance with excessive/chronic misuse.

**Docusate (stool softener) — negative-evidence example:** evidence for meaningful efficacy is surprisingly weak; clinical studies have often failed to show substantial benefit over placebo/alternatives. Do not encode as "proven effective" — correct: "widely used stool softener with limited/low-quality evidence of clinically meaningful benefit."

**Simethicone:** reduces surface tension of gas bubbles, facilitating coalescence/passage; used for gas/bloating/pressure. Mechanism is plausible but clinical evidence for major symptomatic benefit is less impressive than consumer familiarity suggests — plausible mechanism ≠ strongly proven clinical benefit.

**Lactase:** provides exogenous lactase enzyme for lactose intolerance; evidence substantially stronger than many "digestive enzyme" supplements because it directly replaces the deficient enzyme. Effect depends on lactose dose, residual endogenous lactase, timing, formulation.

**Bismuth subsalicylate:** used for diarrhea, nausea, upset stomach, heartburn, some infectious-diarrhea contexts; mechanisms include antisecretory effects, antimicrobial activity, salicylate-mediated effects. AE: black tongue/stool (bismuth sulfide). Contraindication/caution: salicylate allergy, certain anticoagulant/bleeding situations. **Pediatric warning: salicylate-containing products should not be casually used in children/adolescents with viral illness (Reye syndrome risk).**

### PART IV — PAIN & INFLAMMATION

Pharmacy Times 2026: Excedrin Migraine 65% recommendation in its category (not a 65% migraine cure rate).

**Acetaminophen:** predominantly central analgesic/antipyretic; not a peripheral anti-inflammatory NSAID. Strong evidence for headache, fever, mild/moderate acute pain. Vs. NSAIDs: advantages = lower GI toxicity, no platelet inhibition like aspirin, preferable when NSAIDs inappropriate; disadvantages = hepatotoxicity in overdose, narrow safety margin when combined with multiple acetaminophen-containing products. **Most important OTC safety issue: duplicate ingredient exposure** across Tylenol/Tylenol Cold/Tylenol PM/other combos — track total daily acetaminophen exposure across products, not per-package.

**Ibuprofen (NSAID):** COX inhibition → reduced prostaglandin synthesis. Used for pain, fever, inflammation, dysmenorrhea, musculoskeletal pain. Strong RCT evidence for acute pain; vs. acetaminophen, can be similar/superior for some acute pain, and inflammatory conditions generally favor NSAIDs (acetaminophen has little peripheral anti-inflammatory action). Risks: GI bleeding, renal injury, fluid retention, hypertension, cardiovascular risk, bronchospasm in susceptible patients — risk rises with age, kidney disease, anticoagulants, corticosteroids, dehydration, prolonged use.

**Naproxen (NSAID):** longer duration of action than ibuprofen (advantage), but that also means prolonged AE exposure (disadvantage). Same major safety classes as ibuprofen: GI, renal, cardiovascular.

**Aspirin:** unusually multi-purpose — analgesic, antipyretic, anti-inflammatory, irreversible platelet inhibitor. Low-dose aspirin has cardiovascular applications but should not be casually treated as a general OTC preventive medication — modern primary-prevention evidence has substantially narrowed the group for whom benefit outweighs bleeding risk.

**Topical diclofenac (topical NSAID):** among the better-supported topical analgesics, especially for osteoarthritis/localized musculoskeletal pain. Goal: local tissue exposure with reduced systemic exposure vs. oral NSAIDs. Systematic reviews show clinically meaningful pain relief in OA/acute musculoskeletal conditions; diclofenac gel can outperform placebo. AEs more localized (application-site irritation, dermatitis) but systemic NSAID exposure is not zero.

**Excedrin Migraine (acetaminophen + aspirin + caffeine):** combination can provide superior acute migraine relief vs. placebo and some individual components. Mechanistic rationale: acetaminophen (analgesia) + aspirin (analgesia/anti-inflammatory) + caffeine (analgesic adjuvant, may enhance absorption/effect). Drawback: frequent use of acute migraine medication can contribute to medication-overuse headache — store both acute efficacy and max recommended frequency/overuse risk.

### PART V — COUGH, COLD & ALLERGY (ingredient-level, supplementing the brand data already captured)

**Dextromethorphan (antitussive):** centrally acting, sigma-1/NMDA-related pharmacology; used for nonproductive cough. Adult studies show some reduction vs. placebo but inconsistent/modest; evidence considerably weaker in children. Major safety issue: serotonergic activity — potential interaction with MAOIs, serotonergic antidepressants, other serotonergic drugs; large doses can cause dissociation, hallucinations, agitation, toxicity.

**Guaifenesin (expectorant):** intended to increase respiratory-tract fluid/secretions and facilitate mucus clearance, for productive cough/chest congestion. Evidence suggests modest benefit for some acute respiratory symptoms but is "not spectacular" — avoid "clears mucus reliably," prefer "some controlled studies suggest modest improvement in mucus-related symptoms, but clinical effects vary."

**Oral phenylephrine — negative-evidence flagship example:** historically marketed as nasal decongestant, but FDA concluded available data do NOT support oral phenylephrine as effective at recommended OTC doses; FDA proposed removing it from the OTC monograph on lack-of-effectiveness grounds. Textbook example of "historical OTC status ≠ demonstrated efficacy" — do not teach that all decongestants work equally.

**Pseudoephedrine:** substantially stronger evidence than oral phenylephrine for nasal decongestion; mechanism = α-adrenergic vasoconstriction. Benefits: nasal congestion, sinus pressure. Risks: insomnia, palpitations, increased BP, anxiety, urinary retention. Regulated as a methamphetamine precursor (behind-the-counter sales controls).

**Oxymetazoline (topical nasal):** very effective for short-term congestion; major problem is rhinitis medicamentosa/rebound congestion with prolonged use — traditional guidance limits use to ~3 days. One of the most important nasal-spray counseling points.

**Intranasal corticosteroids (fluticasone, triamcinolone, budesonide):** among the most effective OTC therapies for allergic rhinitis. Pharmacy Times: Flonase Allergy Relief leads intranasal allergy products at 77% and intranasal corticosteroid category at 82% (recommendation %, not response rate). Improve nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, sneezing, nasal itching, ocular allergy symptoms; generally outperform oral antihistamines for nasal congestion. AEs: nasal irritation, epistaxis, dryness — technique matters (aim spray away from septum).

**Second-generation antihistamines (cetirizine, levocetirizine, loratadine, desloratadine, fexofenadine):** all block H1 receptors. A 2026 network meta-analysis of 74 RCTs for allergic rhinitis found between-drug differences were mostly small/clinically trivial/low-certainty — prevents the false claim that one is "universally the best." Sedation tendency: fexofenadine very low CNS penetration; loratadine generally low sedation; cetirizine more likely to cause drowsiness than some other second-gen agents; levocetirizine can also cause somnolence — individual-dependent.

**Zinc lozenges:** meta-analyses suggest properly formulated zinc lozenges started early may reduce cold duration, but evidence depends heavily on elemental zinc dose, salt/formulation, timing, frequency, duration. Intranasal zinc is a different story — associated with anosmia, inappropriate for use.

**Homeopathic cold products:** systematic reviews have generally failed to demonstrate convincing efficacy beyond placebo for common cold/flu symptoms at claimed dilutions — classify as "insufficient convincing evidence / no established pharmacologic mechanism at claimed dilution," not equivalent to conventional cold medicine.

### PART VI — FIRST AID

**Topical antibiotics (bacitracin, polymyxin B, neomycin, combinations):** for minor cuts/scrapes. Important point: not always superior to simple wound care with petrolatum — a clean, moist wound environment can heal effectively without antibiotics. Can cause contact dermatitis, especially neomycin. "Antibiotic ointment = automatically better" is incorrect.

**Petrolatum:** underrated first-aid product — maintains moist wound environment, reduces scab formation and desiccation, can support epithelialization. Good example of a simple intervention with strong practical value for uncomplicated minor wounds.

**Topical anesthetics (benzocaine, lidocaine):** for minor pain, itching, oral lesions, superficial skin discomfort. Benzocaine safety: rare but important risk of methemoglobinemia, particularly with excessive use and in infants/young children — not a benign "numbing cream" in all populations.

**Burn treatment (minor thermal burns):** evidence-based first aid centers on cooling with cool running water, removing constrictive objects, protecting the wound, avoiding ice directly on tissue. Topical products are secondary. Avoid butter, grease, irritating household substances.

**Insect bites:** typical treatments — hydrocortisone, antihistamines, calamine, cold compresses — target symptom reduction (itching, swelling, erythema); they do not "neutralize" most insect venom after the fact.

### PART VII — TOPICALS / DERMATOLOGY

**Hydrocortisone 1%:** low-potency topical corticosteroid for eczema, dermatitis, inflammatory itching, insect-bite reactions. Among the best-established inflammatory-dermatosis treatments; important variables are potency, formulation, body site, duration, frequency. AEs with excessive use: skin atrophy, telangiectasia, striae, steroid acne, hypopigmentation, systemic absorption in unusual circumstances — thin-skin areas (face, genitals, folds) more vulnerable.

**Clotrimazole (antifungal):** for tinea pedis/corporis/cruris, superficial candidiasis; inhibits fungal ergosterol synthesis. Topical antifungals are effective for tinea pedis; terbinafine tends to have a particularly strong evidence base. Comparative trial: terbinafine 1 week ~83% effective vs. clotrimazole 4 weeks ~82% (study-defined outcome) — terbinafine's main practical advantage is often shorter treatment duration. Meta-analysis of 19 RCTs/2,899 patients found oral/topical terbinafine substantially better than placebo for mycologic cure.

**Terbinafine topical:** inhibits squalene epoxidase, disrupting fungal cell-membrane synthesis. Strong evidence for dermatophyte infections; advantages = rapid treatment, high cure rates, often shorter course than azoles; disadvantages = not appropriate for every fungal rash (diagnosis matters), may irritate skin.

**Benzoyl peroxide (acne):** antimicrobial activity against Cutibacterium acnes + keratolytic + anti-inflammatory effects. Strong evidence for reducing inflammatory acne lesions; bacterial resistance to benzoyl peroxide itself is not the same problem as with antibiotics, so it's often combined with topical retinoids/antibiotics. AEs: dryness, irritation, peeling, fabric/hair bleaching.

**Salicylic acid:** used for acne, hyperkeratosis, warts, corns/calluses via keratolytic action; evidence varies significantly by indication.

**Wart treatment — salicylic acid:** generally more effective than placebo/no treatment for common warts, but treatment can take weeks and adherence matters. Cryotherapy also works but is associated with pain, blistering, cost.

**CeraVe/moisturizers:** Pharmacy Times 2026 lists CeraVe as leading therapeutic-skincare recommendation at 32% — "CeraVe 32%" does NOT mean "CeraVe improves eczema 32%." Relevant evidence is for ingredients (ceramides, petrolatum, glycerin, humectants); moisturization itself has strong evidence in atopic dermatitis.

### PART VIII — DIABETIC HEALTHCARE

**Diabetic cough products:** more about ingredient selection (sugar-free/alcohol-free formulations for diabetics) than unique efficacy — the actual active drug still determines cough efficacy; "sugar-free" is not automatically clinically superior.

**Diabetic foot care:** primary evidence-based principle is prevention — moisturizing dry skin (avoiding between-toe application when excess moisture is a problem), checking feet daily, proper footwear, avoiding self-treatment of ulcers/calluses in high-risk patients. A cream cannot compensate for neuropathy, vascular disease, infection, or poor wound care.

**Diabetic neuropathy — capsaicin:** activates TRPV1 receptors, causing functional desensitization of nociceptive fibers; evidence supports topical capsaicin for some neuropathic pain. Problems: burning sensation, adherence, variable benefit. High-concentration capsaicin patches are prescription/specialist therapy; OTC creams are lower concentration.

### PART IX — ORAL CARE

**Fluoride toothpaste:** one of the strongest evidence areas in the entire OTC universe — Cochrane reviews consistently find it significantly reduces caries vs. non-fluoride toothpaste; foundational preventive dental care. Effectiveness depends on concentration, brushing frequency, technique, exposure duration, age.

**Potassium nitrate (tooth sensitivity):** reduces nerve excitability; evidence supports potassium-containing desensitizing toothpastes for reducing sensitivity with repeated use.

**Stannous fluoride:** can offer caries prevention + sensitivity reduction + gingivitis/antiplaque effects — useful when multiple dental concerns coexist.

**Hydroxyapatite toothpaste:** emerging, fluoride-free category; Pharmacy Times has specifically discussed hydroxyapatite tablets as fluoride-free alternatives. Evidence suggests remineralization/anti-caries effects, but the evidence base is smaller than fluoride's — "promising" ≠ "same depth of evidence as fluoride."

**Chlorhexidine:** very effective antiseptic mouthwash, but routine OTC-type chronic use is limited by tooth staining, taste disturbance, calculus, mucosal effects — more appropriate for targeted clinical indications than routine daily cosmetic use.

**Benzocaine (canker sores):** temporary local analgesia; does not necessarily accelerate healing or eliminate underlying cause — symptom relief ≠ disease modification.

**Docosanol 10% (cold sores):** OTC for herpes labialis; evidence suggests modest shortening of lesion duration when started early. Practical lesson: early application matters; it is not a cure for HSV.

**Dry mouth — saliva substitutes:** carboxymethylcellulose, glycerin, mucin-like formulations, saliva stimulants — benefits are primarily symptomatic. Underlying xerostomia cause matters (can increase caries, oral infection, dysphagia, oral discomfort) — chronic dry mouth warrants evaluation for medication effects/systemic causes.

### PART X — PEDIATRICS

**Pediatric acetaminophen:** effective antipyretic/analgesic; dose must be weight-based. Major risk: concentration errors and duplicate dosing (historically, differing pediatric liquid concentrations caused significant dosing errors). Always verify weight, concentration, mL dose, dosing interval, max daily dose.

**Pediatric ibuprofen:** effective for fever/pain; often allowed 6 months+ depending on labeling. Avoid/caution in dehydration, renal disease, certain GI conditions.

**Children's cough/cold products:** one of the most important pediatric OTC safety categories — evidence generally shows limited efficacy for many cough/cold ingredients in young children, with risks of dosing errors, sedation, agitation, tachycardia, accidental overdose. Adult formulations should not simply be dose-reduced and given to children.

**Honey (children's cough, >1 year only):** evidence for improving nighttime cough (frequency, severity, sleep) vs. no-treatment/placebo-like controls. **Critical safety restriction: never give honey to infants under 12 months (botulism risk).**

**Homeopathic pediatric cough products:** evidence not convincing; the bigger risk is delaying effective evaluation/treatment while relying on an ineffective product, not merely "it doesn't work."

**Diaper rash — zinc oxide:** barrier protectant reducing moisture exposure and creating a physical barrier; evidence supports barrier creams for prevention/treatment of irritant diaper dermatitis. Most important interventions remain frequent diaper changes, gentle cleansing, minimizing prolonged moisture exposure.

**Infant gas — simethicone:** widely used for infant colic/gas, but randomized evidence is not impressive — several trials fail to show meaningful superiority over placebo. High-use, weak-evidence category; should be prominently encoded as such.

**Children's motion sickness — dimenhydrinate:** antihistamine with anticholinergic properties; can reduce nausea/vomiting/motion-sickness symptoms. AEs: sedation, dry mouth, blurred vision, paradoxical excitation in some children. Dose must be age-specific.

### PART XI — WOMEN'S HEALTH

**Menstrual pain — NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen):** among the best-supported OTC treatments for primary dysmenorrhea, via reduced prostaglandin synthesis (directly targets an important pathophysiologic mechanism). Outperform placebo for pain reduction and functional improvement; generally considered first-line pharmacologic therapy.

**Phenazopyridine (urinary analgesic):** provides symptomatic relief of dysuria, urinary burning, urgency discomfort. **Critical distinction: it does not treat the underlying UTI** — correct framing is "can temporarily reduce urinary discomfort," not "treats a UTI." Persistent urinary symptoms need appropriate evaluation.

**Vaginal lubricants:** reduce friction during sexual activity (water-based, silicone-based, oil-based). Evidence question is symptomatic comfort, not disease treatment. Oil-based products can damage latex condoms.

**Vaginal moisturizers (distinct from lubricants):** lubricant = used around sexual activity; moisturizer = used regularly for baseline vaginal dryness.

**Vaginal antifungals (clotrimazole, miconazole):** treat vulvovaginal candidiasis; effective in uncomplicated yeast infection. Recurrent/atypical symptoms need diagnosis, since bacterial vaginosis, trichomoniasis, dermatitis, and other infections can mimic candidiasis.

**Boric acid:** sometimes used for recurrent vulvovaginal candidiasis, particularly recurrent/non-albicans Candida infections — potential benefit, but toxic if swallowed, not universally appropriate, and a pregnancy safety concern. Formulation and patient population matter enormously here.

### PART XII — DURABLE GOODS

Not primarily a drug-efficacy section — evaluate device performance, adherence, usability, pain, durability, patient outcomes, correct indication (braces, supports, compression products, thermometers, heating/cooling products). The mistake to avoid: importing drug-style efficacy percentages into mechanical devices.

### PART XIII — "SUCCESS RATE" HAS NO UNIVERSAL DEFINITION

Antidiarrheal success might mean no further unformed stool for 24h; antifungal success might mean clinical + mycological cure; antacid success might mean symptom relief at 30/60/120 min; PPI success might mean healed esophagitis at 8 weeks; migraine treatment success might mean pain-free at 2h; acne success might mean % reduction in inflammatory lesions; diagnostic test success might mean sensitivity/specificity vs. reference standard; glucose meter success might mean % of readings within ISO accuracy limits. These should never be collapsed into a single response percentage.

### PART XV — PHARMACY TIMES BRAND DATA THAT MUST STAY SEPARATE FROM EFFICACY

Imodium 81% (antidiarrheals) · Pepcid 83% (H2 blockers) · Flonase Allergy Relief 77% (intranasal allergy) / 82% (intranasal corticosteroids) · Nature Made 66% ("Letter" vitamins) / 51% (CoQ10) · CeraVe 32% (therapeutic skincare) · Excedrin Migraine 65% (migraine). Each should be stored as: source = Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide; metric = pharmacist recommendation percentage; population = practicing pharmacists; n≈1700; meaning = recommendation preference, NOT clinical efficacy.

### PART XVI — CURRENT 2026 SAFETY/RESEARCH FLAGS (from Pharmacy Times' own current coverage)

- **Vitamin D:** phase 3 colorectal-cancer trial — high-dose D3 safe, no significant PFS improvement.
- **Vitamin C:** liposomal formulation improves PK/PD; meaningful immune benefit remains inconclusive.
- **Probiotics:** evidence needs to be matched to specific strains/doses/shelf-life potency, not treated as one category.
- **"GLP-1 boosters":** OTC supplements marketed this way should be separated from actual GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy; marketing claims ≠ clinical evidence.
- **Pediatric OTC:** continued emphasis on careful counseling, avoiding duplicate ingredients in multi-symptom products.
